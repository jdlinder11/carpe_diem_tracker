How to build a habit that sticks

Most habits fail in the first few weeks, not because of weak willpower, but because the plan asked for too much too soon. These seven steps keep the plan small enough to survive a busy week.

The seven steps

  1. Choose one habit. Start with a single habit rather than a full routine. When it feels automatic, add the next one. Several new habits at once compete for the same limited attention.
  2. Make it small and specific. "Exercise more" is a wish. "Walk for ten minutes after lunch" is a habit. A good habit is small enough to do on your worst day and specific enough that you know when it is done.
  3. Attach it to a cue. Link the new habit to something you already do every day: after I pour my coffee, I write one line in my journal. An existing routine is a more reliable reminder than an alarm.
  4. Prepare the environment. Put the book on your pillow, the running shoes by the door, the water bottle on your desk. Remove friction from the habit you want and add friction to the one you don't.
  5. Mark it every day. A daily mark turns an intention into a record. Seeing the row fill in is a small reward, and the gaps show you exactly where the plan needs adjusting.
  6. Plan for missed days. You will miss a day. Decide now what you will do: get back on track the next day, and never miss twice in a row. One gap is a pause. Two gaps start a new pattern.
  7. Review once a week. Look at the week as a whole. If a habit keeps slipping, make it smaller or move it to a better time instead of trying harder.

How long it takes

There is no fixed number of days. A frequently cited study from University College London (Lally and colleagues, 2009) followed people adopting a new daily behavior and found that the time for it to feel automatic varied widely, from about 18 to 254 days, with a median of 66 days. Simple habits became automatic sooner than demanding ones. The same study found that missing a single day did not meaningfully set people back.

The practical lesson: expect a few months, not three weeks, and do not treat one missed day as failure.

Make it easy to start

  • Use a two-minute version. On a low-energy day, do the smallest possible version: one push-up, one page, one line. Showing up keeps the habit alive.
  • Write down when and where. Plans that name a time and place ("at 7:00 in the kitchen") are carried out more often than plans that don't.
  • Keep the cue visible. A tracker you can see each day works as a reminder on its own.

When a habit is not working

  • You keep forgetting. The cue is weak. Tie the habit to a stronger daily anchor.
  • You remember but skip it. The habit is too big. Cut it in half.
  • It feels pointless. The habit may not serve a goal you care about. Replace it rather than forcing it.
  • Weekends break it. Give the habit a separate weekend cue, or decide in advance that it is a weekday habit.
